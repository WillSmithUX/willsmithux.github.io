About

I design for the moment people are most likely to give up.

I'm an HCI researcher and designer completing my M.S. at Indiana University, working across user research, usability testing, and interaction design.

Most of my work circles the same question: what does a system owe someone on their worst day? The user who is tired, behind, or already convinced they've failed. That's the moment interfaces reach for pressure, another streak broken, another number to defend, and it's where careful design earns its keep.

Methodologically I lean empirical. I've run Wizard-of-Oz evaluations instrumented with NASA-TLX, interviewed users across a forty-year age range, tested paper prototypes before building anything, and used value-sensitive design frameworks to audit interfaces for the harms they quietly encode. I'd rather kill an idea with a cheap study than defend it with a polished one.

I came to this from six years of teaching and coaching, where I watched the gap between what people knew and what they could actually do, every day, for years. That's the reason I design for the failure moment, and the reason I founded MindfulWill, where I ship my own tools and test them against real use.

Research

Proposal · LIS-S506 · Summer 2026

The Cognitive Architecture of Machine Consciousness and User Parasociality

Qualitative design · Semi-structured interviews, participant journaling, chat-log narrative analysis

People are forming emotional attachments to conversational AI at scale, and the literature has moved quickly from measuring adoption to measuring dependency. This proposal narrows to a single question: what cognitive work does a person do to sustain the belief that something is there?

The design triangulates three sources against each other: interviews about moments the system felt genuinely self-aware, two weeks of daily journaling on emotional state before and after each session, and participants' own chat logs read as narrative. Coding is inductive and thematic, framed by symbolic interactionism, so that definitions of companion agency emerge from the participants themselves.

I wrote it because the ethics questions I raised about rating systems get sharper when the interface can appear to care back. It stands as a proposal, and the value sits in the method design.
