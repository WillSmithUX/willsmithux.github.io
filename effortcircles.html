Case Study 03 · Product Design

EffortCircles

Designing for gentle accountability, for the people that competition-driven apps quietly push out.

Role
UX researcher & interaction designer
Context
Indiana University · Fall 2025
Methods
User interviews, secondary research, usability testing
Scope
Team of four · 7 interviews, 4 usability sessions

People want accountability. What they don't want is an audience.

Apps like Strava and MyFitnessPal drive engagement through public sharing and competition, and a large share of users eventually disengage. Seven interviews told us why, consistently: health felt personal, comparison felt corrosive, and the emotional context of a workout mattered as much as the numbers.

EffortCircles answers with a two-tier social structure wrapped around a journaling practice. Usability testing validated the core model and caught a labelling problem we were all too close to the design to see.

01

Problem

The dominant pattern in fitness apps assumes visibility motivates: leaderboards, public feeds, streaks on display. For a meaningful share of users the opposite is true. Comparison undermines the personal progress it's meant to fuel, and the moment someone falls behind is precisely when a public metric does the most damage.

So the design question became whether an app could deliver real accountability while removing the audience that makes accountability feel like performance.

“I have always felt like exercising and losing weight is also a competition… at times I don't even enjoy it anymore because I think I am not good enough.”

Interview participant
02

Process

We interviewed seven participants spanning a forty-five year age range, alongside secondary research on app disengagement. Three patterns organised everything that followed:

  • Privacy preference. Participants rarely shared fitness publicly. Health was personal, full stop.
  • Trusted accountability. Sharing felt safe only with small, chosen groups: a friend, a partner, a coach.
  • Emotional context. Motivation, stress, and energy mattered as much as any metric, and no app was capturing them.

Those patterns became the architecture directly. An Inner Circle for honest sharing with a handful of chosen people. An Outer Circle for lower-pressure updates. A binary Save-versus-Post action so the privacy decision is explicit rather than buried in settings. Calm colour and minimal required inputs, so journaling on a bad day costs almost nothing. And energy and stress as first-class fields, because that's what participants said they were actually tracking.

We wireframed in Balsamiq, built high-fidelity flows in Figma, then ran four moderated usability sessions against the core tasks: write an entry, save it privately, share it to a circle. My focus across the team was the research synthesis and the interaction model for the circles themselves.

03

Findings

The heart of the design held up. Every participant successfully saved and shared an entry once inside the flow, which told me the Inner/Outer Circle model and the binary Save-versus-Post choice were legible without explanation. Nobody hesitated over who would see what, the thing I'd been most worried about.

The failure was at the front door. Three of four participants selected Journal when asked to write an entry, even though we'd put that action under Reflections. Users had cleanly divided the space into writing (Journal) and reviewing (Reflections), and our label violated that model. The fix was consolidation: all writing actions live under Journal, and Reflections becomes what users already assumed it was, the archive.

The design lever for accountability is control over who is watching.

Synthesis · research patterns 1 to 3

The broader insight is that emotional safety is structural. I could have written warmer microcopy all day and it would have moved nothing. What made participants willing to engage were architectural decisions: who can see this, how deliberately I choose, and whether the system asks me to compete with anyone. Those are information-architecture problems wearing an emotional costume.

04

Reflection

EffortCircles taught me to treat labels as testable hypotheses rather than naming decisions. Journal versus Reflections was invisible from inside our own mental model, obvious within four sessions, and a reminder that the cheapest research usually catches the thing a team is least able to see on its own.

It also sharpened the theme running through all of my work: behavior change sticks when systems reduce pressure. The honest limitation is that this was evaluated as a concept, in single sessions. The claim that gentle accountability sustains engagement past the point where competitive apps lose people is exactly the sort of claim that needs a longitudinal study, and that's the version of this project I'd want to run next.
